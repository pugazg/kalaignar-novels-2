Romapuri was a land made beautiful by a rain of light from heroic lamps such as the mighty Pompey; Caesar, who made mighty warriors kneel; Antony, the lion reared by Caesar; and Augustus, who subdued Antony.

How it lived and the story of how it declined are no secret. History laments and explains that the dark-lined eyes of courtesans and wine cups were the causes of that sacred land's great fall. Once, with rise and splendour swaying, that tiny charming golden country spread its fame in all eight directions like a lamp lit upon a hill. Historians calculate and say that the time when its lustre faded and it became like a veena knocked about and thrown into the dust was A.D. 1453.

Whatever person deserves the honour of first introducing the Roman Empire to the world, the full honour of giving birth to that infant belongs to the man called Romulus.

In 753 B.C., a group speaking the Latin language entered Italy. They founded a small town on the bank of the Tiber. Seven hills surrounded it, with the town in the middle. The leader of the group that founded the town was Romulus. His own name came to be given to that town.

After encountering many dangers, Romapuri gradually raised its breast and rose, determined to stand upright before the eyes of the world. Those who gave force to that rise were the strong and mighty heroes Pompey, Caesar, Antony, Augustus and others like them!

Pompey's head was cut off on the sands of the Egyptian coast—but on that same stretch of sand, the great beauty Cleopatra enjoyed delight with her head resting on Caesar's lap! This happened around 48 B.C. Some Roman nobles stabbed that Caesar to death near Pompey's statue. After that, the beautiful queen of Egypt, Cleopatra, became Antony's. Caesar's adopted son Octavius began to develop the desire to bring Romapuri under his own domination—

and as a result the members of the Roman parliament proclaimed Antony an enemy.

The war flag reached the sky with the colour of blood! The two great warriors entered the battlefield. The battlefield gave Antony, who was rolling in battle, no encouragement. He turned his back and withdrew. Octavius placed the jeweled crown upon his head as the first king of kings of Romapuri! The Roman parliament joyfully bestowed upon Octavius the title "Augustus" as an abbreviated name for the heroic phrase "the glorious great warrior"!

Augustus Caesar stood roaring like a lion—while Antony lay curled up in the women's quarters. The time of this event may be placed around 30 B.C.

It was in that period, historians have determined, that the victorious shadow of the flags of the three great royal houses of our ancient Tamilakam flourished, and that in the Chola country Thirumavalavan, the second Karikala Peruvallathan, was ruling. Tamil scholars state that the period of this Karikalan was from 60 B.C. to 10 B.C.

There are also records that a Pandiyan king sent his envoy to Augustus Caesar, who held sway in Romapuri, and that this was in 20 B.C.

If so, who was the Pandiyan king who sent that envoy to King Augustus of Romapuri?

Those who have tried to answer this question have so far offered many kinds of confusion as their answer!

Karikala Peruvallathan had another name, "Kuraappallith Thunjiya Perunthirumavalavan". This name was given after his death. He received it because he died at a place called Thiruvidaikkazhi or Kuraappalli. A poet called Karikannanar of Kaveripoompattinam, who sang a *puram* poem about him, joined a Pandiyan king with him in that poem. That king's name was Peruvazhuthi. After his death, he was called "Velliyambalaththu Thunjiya Peruvazhuthi"!

"You are the king of Uraiyur, where righteousness abides; he is the ruler with the cool sceptre in Madurai, where Tamil gathers! You belong to the cool waters of the Kaveri—

<!-- source boundary: scan 24 → scan 25 -->

and he is like a bull of the Pandiyan clan, glorious in war, possessing the sandalwood of Podhigai hill, the pearls of the sounding sea and the three drums. You two appear like two great gods standing together! May our friendship endure forever as it does today! May your spear triumph again and again and rise high on the battlefield! Engrave the tiger and the fish, and carve your victory upon the hills of other lands!"

Giving this beautiful thought in the fifty-eighth *Puram* poem, Karikannanar praises Cholan Karikalan and Pandiyan Peruvazhuthi together.

From this, we conclude that the Pandiyan of Karikalan's time was Peruvazhuthi. Karikalan's period was from 60 B.C. to 10 B.C.

It was in that same period that Augustus Caesar was exercising power in Romapuri—that is, from 39 B.C. to A.D. 14.

From examining this calculation of dates, we are led to the conclusion that the Pandiyan who maintained friendly relations with Augustus Caesar and sent him an envoy must have been Peruvazhuthi.

Trade relations had arisen between Romapuri and the south even before this period, and the people of the two lands mingled and rejoiced together. After Karikalan and Peruvazhuthi Pandiyan assumed their crowns, the Roman connection began to flourish and grow rapidly. The relationship that rose high in the time of Augustus Caesar lasted for many years. It may be said that only after the reign of King Nero did that relationship gradually wear away and disappear.

Archaeological investigations stand in support as evidence of the connections that existed between Tamil country and Rome.

Literature bears witness that the Greeks and Romans were called Yavanas and that Tamil kings kept those Yavanas in their palaces as guards. A line from the Silappathikaram—"Yavanas of fierce sword, distinguished in guarding the gate of the strong-walled fort"—is one such example.

In the fifty-sixth *Puram* poem sung by Nakkiranar, the line "the cool fragrant wine brought in fine vessels by the Yavanas" indicates that the Yavanas brought varieties of wine to Tamilakam and sold them there.

Among the food products exported from the south to Romapuri, pepper was especially important. It is said that when Alaric once conquered Rome, the agreement for compensation required that three thousand pounds of Tamil-country pepper also be given. <!-- verified Tamil split-word boundary: scan 25 → scan 26 --> Pearls dived for by prisoners sentenced to death at a place called Kulachal, south of Musiri, were also exported to Rome.

Ptolemy writes down the name of one Tamil port connected with Rome as the port of "Kolavaris"; scholars say that he may have been referring to Kaveripoompattinam by that name. He also mentions a port called "Pudogi". They say that it was probably Puduvai. Near the great port of Korkai in the Pandiyan country, pearls and conches were found in great abundance and were praised by people in all parts of the world. That there is no equal to the pearl of Korkai is shown by the lines:

"Korkai, guarded in righteousness by the warlike Pandyas,  
and the pearls of its great harbour."

So an *agam* poem beautifully explains.

Pearls, fine cotton cloth, and garments woven from rat hair and silk were sent to countries such as Romapuri; from there horses, varieties of wine and glassware continually arrived and were unloaded at the great port of Korkai.

It is said that the Yavanas would come in beautiful ships laden with gold and then return carrying pepper. Sangam literature proudly says that the great roar of those ships could be heard throughout the port of Musiri.

Tamil literature says that merchants and sailors from all countries would arrive at the port of Puhar and that they would all mingle in unity as though they belonged to one people. The single fact that many Yavanas also served as bodyguards to Tamil kings is itself a mirror revealing the goodwill that existed between the two sides.

As though proving such connections before us today, excavations at a place called Arikamedu near the city of Puduvai have uncovered many kinds of objects belonging to Romans. In Tamilakam one can find many of the vessels, lamps, coins and other things they used. At Arikamedu, some boys discovered a valuable stone bearing the image of Augustus Caesar. But it is said that it has now disappeared.

<!-- source boundary: scan 26 → scan 27 -->

Roman gold coins were discovered at sixty-eight places, including locations in India and Pakistan. When we hear that fifty-seven of those sixty-eight places lie south of the Vindhyas, we sigh deeply as we think again and again of the old relationship that existed between Rome and the south.

Most of those coins belong to the periods of Augustus and a man called Tiberius! Coins of Nero's period, like Nero himself, are seen to be of poorer quality, with silver mixed into the gold. History says that Tiberius, speaking in the Roman parliament, condemned the outflow of Roman money to foreign countries.

People investigated why heaps upon heaps of Roman coins were found in Coimbatore district. To send goods from the eastern coast to Romapuri by way of the western coast, one either had to go around Kanyakumari or travel through the cross-route where the Eastern Ghats and Western Ghats meet and leave a passage. Some also give this as a reason for the large number of coin hoards found in that region today.

In one way or another, abundant evidence that Tamilakam and Rome may have had relations of every kind has been obtained through archaeology, through literature and from history.

It is with this old connection as my centre that I have taken courage to create 'Romapuri Pandiyan'.

The period of Augustus Caesar: 39 B.C. to A.D. 14! The period of Karikalan: 60 B.C. to 10 B.C. Pandiyan Peruvazhuthi was not only Karikalan's friend. He maintained friendship with Rome—he was the one who sent an envoy.

Augustus and Antony have met on the battlefield and returned! Antony's end has not yet been told; Cleopatra's tragic end has not yet been written! Nor has it been announced where our imaginary heroine is going to come from. Is she truly an imaginary heroine? Or is she a maiden hidden in the lines of a history book who has never yet been introduced? Who is the Romapuri Pandiyan? What meaning is this strange title going to make clear?

Is this too not a riddle? Let it be! Let it be!  
For now, it is enough if the relations between Romapuri and Tamilakam, the dates, and the names of contemporary kings are known without doubt. They have now become clear!

Now, may we continue the story?
