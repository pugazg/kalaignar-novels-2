Where could they have taken Sezhiyan? What might they have done to him?

It was all trouble she had brought upon herself! If she had not opened the door in order to pay respect to the Siva devotees, would any of this have happened?

Worrying like this, that tender young woman grew utterly exhausted. Time rolled by until midnight. Everywhere there was a terrifying silence. The palace bell announced the stroke of midnight and then fell silent. As the lighthouse on the Poompuhar shore revolved, its light flashed "palich, palich" across the courtyard of her house and vanished again and again; it seemed an apt image of her state of mind.

Sensing that someone was lighting a lamp inside the darkened house, she turned and looked. It was the poet who had lit it. Muthunagai continued watching him. Sitting beneath the lamp, the poet took palm-leaf manuscripts in hand and began driving his stylus across them. Muthunagai wondered what he could be writing. She answered her own question: it must be some poem.

Then came the sound of a gentle knock at the street door! Muthunagai rose and ran toward it.

"Stop! Don't go! I'll open it!" the poet said as he came toward the door with the stylus still in his hand. From the very way he held it, it was clear that he meant to use the stylus in self-defence if an enemy should be outside.

The door opened. An elderly man entered, his beard and moustache thick and full. He wore a long dark-blue robe. Over it lay an embroidered upper cloth. A piece of red silk was wrapped around his head.

"What, poet! Are you well?" the old man asked as he came in.

Muthunagai had seen the old man before. She had even spoken with him. Karikannanar had told Muthunagai that he was a man of the Yavana country, a great merchant prince, and one who was very fond of him. <!-- source boundary: scan 53 → scan 54 --> She too bowed to the old man and welcomed him.

The old man came close to Muthunagai and, affectionately stroking her head, asked, "Are you well, child?"

"I am well, grandfather! What brings you here at this hour?" Muthunagai asked, looking at him.

"He must have had some business; that is why he has come. Is that even a question? Muthu! Go and lie down and sleep!" the poet said, and she went to her bed.

After that, the old man and the poet spoke about some secret matter for a long time. Muthunagai made a great effort to hear somehow what they were discussing. Sharpening her ears, she strained toward the direction from which their voices came. She could catch only a few scattered words: "Chola country", "Pandiyan realm", "Velir people". The word "Sezhiyan" too was spoken from time to time. Whenever it was, she struggled hard to grasp the whole sentence around it. But it was of no use.

At last, the Yavana elder rose to take his leave. The poet took two or three palm leaves from the manuscript and gave them to him. He received them and put them away safely.

The old man slowly crossed the threshold and walked out. The poet too went as far as the street and saw him off. Wondering whether they might be saying anything while standing in the street, Muthunagai went toward the doorway to listen. Something near the door struck her foot. She bent down, picked it up, and went near the lamp to look at it. A small medallion! A medallion set all around with diamonds! The letters at its centre drew her eyes. She looked closely. "Irungovel" was written there. She could not believe her eyes. She read it again and again. The same letters remained. They did not change for her.

<!-- source boundary: scan 54 → scan 55 -->

"King Irungovel? The enemy of this country? Then is this man not a Yavana elder? Alas! Is father maintaining ties with the enemy?"

The questions pierced and battered her heart. Hiding the medallion, she went and lay down on her bed.

After seeing the old man off, the poet bolted the door and lay down to sleep.
