4

The Poet's Daughter
Sets Out

<!-- source boundary: scan 67 → scan 68 -->

Dawn broke. The poet awoke. Shaking off his weariness, he rose from bed and looked around. He did not see the clear *neeragaram* that was usually kept in a silver vessel beside his bed. Ever since his youth, the poet had been accustomed to rinsing his mouth on waking and drinking *neeragaram*. Until that day there had never been any interruption to the habit. The change that morning bewildered him.

He looked at the place where Muthunagai had slept. She was not there. He turned toward the street. The door stood open. He trembled with a shock like that of a man who, lost in writing, had driven his own stylus into his head.

"Muthunagai! Muthunagai!" he cried, running all through the house. He went out toward the garden and shouted. His agitation did not subside. He ran to the well. Only the wooden pulley hung over the well-head; the coil of rope was nowhere to be seen. "Ayyo, Amma!" he began to cry, but then, as though appearing directly before his eyes, he saw the rope and the water-drawing vessel, and felt relieved.

He went inside again and stood staring intently at the place where she had slept. A palm leaf was peeping out near the pillow. Brimming with anger and urgency, he picked it up and looked at it. His ears could not believe the words his own tongue uttered. His knowledge of language refused to accept the letters that met his eyes. The palm leaf thundered out such terrifying sentences like a calamity. It made them appear like a flash of storm-lightning. He shut his eyes tightly. Drops of water peered out through the gaps between his eyelids. Unable to believe himself, he read it once again.
