They leapt at one another again and again and fought. The warriors seated upon them fought in a frenzy, spears in hand, as though each were trying to take the other's life first. The poet watched them closely. He could recognize one of them. It was the warrior from the Pandiyan country who had ridden on horseback beside the Pandiyan Peruvazhuthi in the procession.

Who was the other man? He could not tell. Yet he too was dressed in the garb of a Pandiyan warrior.

Those who had gathered watched every moment, wondering how this fight would end. One sprang like a tiger; the other roared like a lion. If the two men's spears met and struck each other point against sharp point, how skilled they must have been at taking careful aim and killing an enemy! At last, a warrior fell in the street, his chest exposed to the point of a spear. The warrior who had come with Peruvazhuthi tore open the fallen man's clothing and looked. What he wore on the outside was indeed the dress of the Pandiyan country; underneath was his true attire. It became clear that he was Villavan, the commander of the petty ruler Irungovel, an enemy of the Chola king.

The enemy commander had fallen dead. The warrior who had brought him down, faint with exhaustion from the fight, slumped against the steps of the poet's house.

In agitation, the poet took him inside and tried to revive him. There were severe wounds on his chest. Blood was seeping from a wound on his forehead made by the enemy's spear. The poet and Muthunagai hurried about tending to him.

After a short while he recovered from his faint and sat up. He could not bear the pain of his wounds. He writhed and sagged with his whole body. Muthunagai applied boiled turmeric to the blunt wounds. The great poet searched out various medicines and applied them to the bleeding wounds.

"Who are you, son?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 35 → scan 36 -->

"I am from the Pandiyan country, a servant of King Peruvazhuthi!"

"Your name?"

"Sezhiyan."

"Who was the man who fell in the fight?"

"He was an enemy of the Chola king—a traitor who had come to kill Karikala Valavan—the commander Villavan of the petty ruler Irungovel.

"The coward had come to attack the Chola king from behind as he passed in the procession and then run away and hide! I saw him just as he was about to hurl his spear at King Karikalan. I slipped quietly away from the procession and followed him. He realized what I was doing and began to run. I chased him and cornered him here. The traitor is finished!" Sezhiyan said with a deep breath.

"Thank goodness—our king escaped! Warrior! May Mother Kaveri bless you! Brave son of Mother Tamil! I marvel at your prowess. Until the day I die I shall not forget how you sprang like a *vengai* and won this victory. The people of the city of Puhar are bound to thank you. You saved our king; I shall go at once and arrange for a fitting reward to be presented to you in the king's august presence!" cried the great poet, who poured out Tamil in abundance, almost leaping with joy.

"No, poet! No! Neither king should learn that such a thing happened. At a time when a festival of friendship is being celebrated, it is not good for reports to spread that attempts are being made to disrupt it. Please let this news die with us. Let it go no further than this: two warriors fought, one died, one survived!" Sezhiyan pleaded.

Just then someone called from the entrance, "Great poet! Great poet!"

The poet went toward the street. Two palace guards stood there and bowed to him.

<!-- source boundary: scan 36 → scan 37 -->

"The king has ordered us to bring you," said the guards. Then, with alarm, they asked, "There is a corpse lying in the street. What happened?"

"Yes. Two warriors from the Pandiyan country fought each other with spears. One of them fell. That is all. I am going to the king; you arrange to have this body removed," said the poet. He came back inside and told Sezhiyan the news. Sezhiyan too took leave and tried to rise, but he could not get up.

The poet held him and made him sit. "Stay here! Muthunagai will look after you. I shall go to the palace and return at once!" he said, and set out.

Sezhiyan could not argue and stop him. The man was a great poet, and the pain was unbearable; so he accepted it and remained seated.

"Muthunagai! Look after him carefully, child!" the poet said, and went out.

He climbed into the horse-drawn palanquin carriage waiting in the street. The beautiful carriage set off toward the palace.

Karikalan and Peruvazhuthi came forward to receive the poet and seated him. The great poet rejoiced at seeing the two together. When he thought of the danger that had nearly befallen Karikalan and of how it had been averted, an involuntary fear shook him.

Without showing it, he asked, "What is the reason you sent for me?"

"We called you only to bless our friendship!" said the jewel of the Cholas.

The poet closed his eyes for a moment and remained silent. His lips moved. "Long live Tamil!" he said. Then a rain of Tamil followed.

"You are the lord
of cool-watered Kaveri; and this one..."

he began to sing.

<!-- source boundary: scan 37 → scan 38 -->

"O just king of Tamil-rich Koodal,
who rules with the three resounding drums,
where rice and water are easy for all,
with sandalwood from the hills and pearls from the sea!"

"Enemies will try in many ways to break your unity. Speaking as though they are friends, as though they are good men, they will scheme to destroy the harmony between you. Give them no room; remain friends with one another!" he advised. The poet spoke so because the event that had occurred in front of his own house was still in his mind.

"May your relationship flourish always as it does today! May our spears win victory after victory and cast the light of fame upon the battlefield! May our tiger and fish be carved upon the hills of enemy lands!"

As the poet opened his heart in this way, the two kings embraced him and shed tears of joy.

Delighting in the embrace of the emperors, the great poet said in a trembling voice, "Long live Tamil! May the motherland win ever greater victories!" His eyes, without his realizing it, became pools of tears. "A desire I have cherished for many days has been fulfilled today. The Velir chieftains, the Oli Nadar, and the common folk who live in the intermediate lands, all of whom have been waiting eagerly for the Pandiyas and the Cholas to become enemies so that they might seize the right moment to wage war, must be embracing disappointment on hearing of today's festival," Karikannanar thundered with his eyes wide and a new energy in his voice.

"All this is the result of your King Karikalan's political wisdom in action," said the Pandiyan king with a smile.

"No, no! It is nothing of the sort... Something was meant to happen; it happened, that is all!" said Karikalan.

The poet spoke proudly: "Why should my king conceal it? The country knows, and I know, that no number of palm-leaf records would be enough to write in praise of your political wisdom. Not merely today—even the deeds you achieved in your youth are still celebrated by assemblies of scholars as lofty deeds beyond comparison, and hearing them cools my heart. Guardian of Madurai! Listen while I give you one small example of our Chola king's ability and political wisdom!

"In the Chola king's youth, two elderly men came to the hall of justice to present a grievance. Seeing the king's youthful appearance, the old men were dismayed: could this young man hear their plea and deliver justice? Seeing Karikalan's age, they dismissed him so lightly. Thinking that they would not receive justice from a boy, they decided to turn back. Somehow the king understood. He told the old men who had come to petition that he would bring someone fully qualified to give them proper justice, and went away. Later the court assembled. The old petitioners came. An old man, tottering and leaning on a staff, sat upon the throne and began the hearing. The case ended. A good judgment was delivered. Pleased by that just decision, the petitioners rejoiced and blessed the old man. The old man's beard fell away. The grey hair came off. The truth was revealed: the man who had come in the disguise of an old man and delivered justice was the young Karikalan himself. The country rejoiced and blessed Karikalan. Such is the ability of our king," the poet said in praise.

Karikalan, unwilling to hear praise of himself, hung his head. The Pandiyan too joined the poet and began to garland him with praise.

"I know as well the cruel men who, when Karikalan was young, threw that lion into prison amid the confusion created by his enemies and plotted to drink his life. The enemies set fire to the prison fortress. Had he not escaped from that blazing fire, Tamilakam would have lost a great warrior; I would never have gained a friend worthy of reverence; and the splendour of Poompuhar would have vanished long ago."

Seeing the Pandiyan and the poet taking turns to speak about him, Karikalan said gently, "Enough! Please stop! This jewelled crown already makes my head heavy; do not make it heavier still!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 39 → scan 40 -->

"Your head hangs because it has grown too heavy for your neck to hold, my king! How much humility keeps filling that head of yours!" said the poet.

"You are a flowering vine that spreads the fragrance of Tamil! The Pandiyan is the guardian of classical Tamil, who lets that fragrance spread everywhere and builds a fence to protect the vines! When both of you have praised me, it is as though Mother Tamil herself has embraced me, kissed me, and blessed me. What more could I lack?" Karikalan said, pouring out the feelings of his heart.

While the guardian of Tamil and the poet were swimming in the flood of Tamil released by their open hearts, a Greek guard serving in the palace's inner precinct came before King Karikalan, bowed, and stood upright.

"What is it?" Karikalan asked.

"An incident took place almost the moment the kings' procession ended. Two warriors from the Pandiyan country fought each other. One died; no one knows where the other went. They say one of the two was named Sezhiyan."

Before the guard had finished, Peruvazhuthi Pandiyan trembled. "Ah! Sezhiyan? Was the dead man's name Sezhiyan? Guard, tell me clearly! Was the dead man Sezhiyan?" Peruvazhuthi asked urgently.

"I do not know which of the two was Sezhiyan. But they say the fight took place directly opposite the poet's house. The dead man's body has been laid in the ruined pavilion behind the palace. If the king wishes to see it, arrangements have been made," the guard replied.

The poet was utterly shaken. Two or three times he moved his tongue as though he might tell them what had happened. But no words came out. Sezhiyan had asked him not to tell anyone the news. He decided silence was best.

<!-- source boundary: scan 40 → scan 41 -->

King Karikalan suddenly asked the poet, "They say it happened right opposite your house. Do you know nothing at all about it?"

"But I came here, did I not?" said the poet.

"Alas—Sezhiyan must not have died! He must not have died! Chola king! I must go at once to see that warrior's body; I must learn who has died!" Peruvazhuthi pleaded in agitation.

"I shall make the arrangements at once. I will come too!" Karikalan replied, and ordered the guard to arrange it.

The poet folded his hands and took leave. He knew that Sezhiyan, who was dear to the Pandiyan, had not died. So there was no change in his face. Karikalan rose with the poet, walked some distance to see him off, then took the Pandiyan with him and hurried toward the ruined pavilion behind the palace. Peruvazhuthi Pandiyan's heart seemed to run ahead of him toward the pavilion. Passing through many beautiful parts of the palace, the two kings walked toward the designated pavilion.

As they went, Karikalan asked the warriors nearby, "Has a guard been posted around the pavilion?"

The warrior-chief said, "It is only a corpse, sire—where can it run off to? Still, just in case, four or five men are standing guard."

The kings approached the pavilion. They were startled. The five warriors guarding it lay fallen with terrible wounds—dead.

Karikalan saw the horrifying sight. "We came to see only one corpse, did we not?" he asked in alarm. Unable to contain his anger, he rushed into the pavilion.

Inside lay a covered body. It was draped in the clothing of the Pandiyan country. "Pandiyan king, see whether this is <!-- source boundary: scan 41 → scan 42 -->the Sezhiyan you spoke of!" Karikalan said and pulled away the cloth covering the body. There was indeed a corpse beneath it. But it was not the lifeless body of a man. It was the rotting carcass of a mule!

Both kings recoiled in shock. They looked at one another. Karikalan did not know what to say or whom to blame.

"How did this disgrace happen? Who allowed it?" he raged.

All the warriors stood trembling. The Chola king called the Greek guard closer.

"You saw that warrior's body, did you not?"

"I did, sire!"

"You were the one who placed that corpse safely in this ruined pavilion, were you not?"

"I and the other warriors did, sire!"

"Then how did a mule come here in place of the warrior?"

"That is the mystery, my king!"

"Cowards! Blind fools! The enemy has played his game inside our own fortress—and you let it happen and were deceived, you fools!"

Karikalan's anger went beyond all bounds. Turning to the Pandiyan, he asked softly, "My friend, forgive me!" Fire was still flying from his eyes.

"It is enough for me if I receive the happy news that the dead man is not Sezhiyan!" Peruvazhuthi Pandiyan said, his eyes filling with tears.

"Who is this Sezhiyan? You seem deeply worried about him!" the Chola king asked.

<!-- source boundary: scan 42 → scan 43 -->

"Sezhiyan—his very name is sweet honey to me. A rich son of Tamil! A bull of the battlefront! A thinker! A young poet who sings pictorial verse! I cannot live without him—he is my life!"

The Chola king sank into wonder at the portrait the Pandiyan painted of Sezhiyan. "What is the relationship between the two of you?" Karikalan asked as he began to leave the pavilion.

"The full story about him is a long tale. I shall tell you another time. For now, all I want is news of him!" said the Pandiyan.

At once Karikalan summoned his warriors and ordered them to bring word immediately even if they found the slightest information about Sezhiyan. Karikalan promised the Pandiyan that he would take full responsibility for the matter.

"The exhaustion from the festival, and the worry that followed it, may have disturbed your peace of mind. Please rest in the mansion that has been arranged for you," Karikalan requested. The Pandiyan agreed, parted from Karikalan, and set off toward the separate mansion.
