6

The Woodcutter

<!-- source boundary: scan 95 → scan 96 -->

All her work lay in that forest region.

She travelled a long distance along the edge of the forest, dismounted from her horse, and looked in all four directions. She saw a ruined mandapam and, beside it, a beautiful pond. It seemed like a place where she could bathe. She decided that it was the safest place for a bath. Tying the horse in a concealed spot, she removed her male clothes, left them on the bank and stepped into the pond. The cool water relieved not only her weariness but even the irritation in her mind. With no desire to come ashore, she swam and played in the water. Wondering how many more days she would have to wander about hiding the radiance of her womanhood beneath men's clothing, she admired the beauty of her own body submerged in the water. When she thought of a woman falling in love with her, laughter rose in her without her realizing it. She laughed aloud.

She did not notice that two eyes were watching that blissful bath through a gap in the bushes. She bathed without concern. Ashamed to watch her bathing, those eyes closed and opened again. Then the eyes moved. After that, they disappeared from the place.

After remaining in the water for a long while, Muthunagai came ashore. With neither fear nor embarrassment, she stood on the bank and changed her clothes. She believed that no one but herself had seen her beautiful form—her mango-like body that could enchant any beholder—or her reflection dancing in the water.

After carefully putting on her male clothes, she came to the mandapam on the bank. She started in surprise. There, a rough-looking man was cooking, with a pot set upon a hearth made by stacking a few bricks. On seeing him <!-- source boundary: scan 96 → scan 97 -->she stood completely still. She consoled herself with the thought that he could not have seen her, or the state in which she had been bathing.

He had turned the other way and was deeply occupied with his cooking. Only after hearing the sound of her approaching footsteps did he slowly turn around. Ah! What a striking face! A large, carefully twisted moustache! Broad shoulders! A distinctive gleam in his eyes that could captivate anyone! A well-built body! He was neither a youth nor an old man; one could not quite call him a middle-aged young man. Nor could one say that he was no longer youthful!

The moment he saw her, he rose like a frightened man and stood respectfully. She too gestured for him to sit. Without sitting down, he remained standing and studied her face. Afraid that her femininity might somehow become visible, Muthunagai kept touching her face and partly covering it with her hands.

Then he looked at her and asked, "Who are you?"

She made signs to show that she was mute.

He did not understand, but he was eager to understand. "What place are you from?" he asked.

Again he did not understand the signs she made. "What's your name?" he asked.

She took up the pearl at her neck and showed it to him, making him understand that this was her name.

"Oh, so your name is Muthu?" he asked. Then he welcomed her: "Looks like you've travelled a long way! Sit down... you can have some of the pongal I'm cooking."

Muthunagai shook her head to say no, though the torment of hunger made her feel that she could eat a little. She rubbed her stomach.

"Aha! Your stomach's asking for it! Sit down!" the man said. With cheerful familiarity, he too rubbed her stomach, caught her by the hand and made her sit.

<!-- source boundary: scan 97 → scan 98 -->

The water in the pot on the hearth was boiling furiously. He poured in the cashew nuts and lumps of jaggery that he had tied up in a piece of cloth, and stirred them into the pot. The pongal was ready. He took the pot down, ran out of the mandapam, plucked two large poovarasu leaves and returned. He washed them in the pond, gave one leaf to her, and served pongal from the pot onto it. He put pongal on the other leaf for himself and began to eat.

Seeing his hospitable nature, Muthunagai felt pleased within herself. However poor the household might be, when it came to receiving a guest, there was no land equal to Tamil Nadu but Tamil Nadu itself, she told herself. If she said it aloud, her disguise would be exposed!

"Hm... have a little more, sir!" he said, serving more pongal onto her leaf.

It did no good when Muthunagai gestured, "Enough!"

"You haven't even asked who I am—I'm a woodcutter, name's Veeran! No mother, no father. An orphan! I have to cut the firewood myself, carry it myself, sell it myself—that's the whole of my story!"

Saying this, he put still more pongal on Muthunagai's leaf. She shook her head to refuse and set the leaf down.

"Come on! Just eat, sir! It's so tasty! Here... take this," he said, scooping up some pongal and stuffing it into her mouth.

Muthunagai could neither chew nor swallow! Not the pongal—the truth about herself!

With great difficulty she finished eating the pongal, stepped into the pond and washed her hands. As Muthunagai washed, the medallion tucked at her waist fell down. She immediately picked it up and secured it safely. It was the very medallion inscribed "Irungovel" that the Yavana elder had dropped!

<!-- source boundary: scan 98 → scan 99 -->

Poor Muthunagai did not know the truth: the Irungovel to whom that medallion belonged was the very man who had cooked pongal for her, satisfied her hunger and shown her hospitality. When she finished washing her hands and came ashore, Irungovel, still in the guise of the woodcutter, offered her tamboolam. She accepted it and put it in her mouth.

"Oh! Sir's mouth has gone nicely red!" said Irungovel, pinching her red lips with his rough fingers. Though she did not like it, the sharp pain that began when his fingers pinched her lips somehow passed away. She managed to compose herself.

However hard Muthunagai tried to turn her eyes away from Irungovel's striking figure in the woodcutter's guise, she failed every time.

His tall, firm shoulders and broad, expansive chest greatly attracted her. Good heavens! What enormous eyes he had! She delighted in thinking that those eyes were like a standing mirror in which she might look at her own face, place a beauty mark upon it and make herself radiant.

Standing beside him, she looked like a fawn at the foot of a mountain; if he were to scoop her up and begin fondling her, it would look like a parakeet sitting in the hollow of a massive banyan branch!

Fortunately, nothing of that sort happened. Enduring the pain he had caused in her lip, she began to move away from the mandapam.

"What, sir, leaving already? Stay a while!" said Irungovel, catching her hand and pulling her back.

"Hush! No!" she gestured, wrenching her hand free and trying to leave.

"No! That's not happening! I won't let you go," Irungovel cried with a laugh. With his rough arms he scooped her up bodily, spun himself around three <!-- source boundary: scan 99 → scan 100 -->times in a whirl, carried her into the mandapam and dropped her down with a thump.

Dizzy from being spun around and around, she lay faint and unable to open her eyes. He became frightened.

"Muthu! Muthu! Sir! Sir!" he called.

He ran to the pond, brought water and sprinkled it over her. He laid her in his lap and rubbed her chest. At that moment his whole body shivered and trembled! A body trembles in the cold, of course; but he knew that it could tremble even in such heat. The woman who had been lying unconscious suddenly sat up with a start.

Muthunagai understood that the woodcutter, believing her to be a man, was behaving with such abandon that the situation was being drawn somewhere beyond control. She could neither like that situation nor hate it. Pulling the cloth of her turban across her chest, she sat a little farther away from him.

Her fear and suspicion that he might have discovered the truth about her increased. She watched his face. She could read no sign at all from that valour-filled face. Irungovel too was born of the great Tamil stock whose code of valour held that it was cowardice even to blink when spears came flying straight at one's eyes! Could one so easily detect changes of feeling and expression on his face?

"How are you? Still dizzy?" Irungovel asked.

Muthunagai shook her head to mean "No."

She did not say, "The dizziness has begun in my heart!" Nor did her eyes entirely fail to say it. It seemed that he too had understood.

"Were you born mute?" he asked.

At once she scribbled rapidly on a palm leaf and held it out before him. It was the very same sentence she had written and shown to Thamarai!

<!-- source boundary: scan 100 → scan 101 -->

"What's this? Looks like a woman's handwriting! Give me your finger. Ah, yes! Even the finger's like a little bean pod," Irungovel teased, and bit her finger!

She writhed, unable to bear the pain. Yet she did not become angry. She put the bitten finger into her mouth and kept moistening it with her tongue so that the blood would not clot.

Only then did she remember that, without wiping it, she had put into her mouth a finger that had been touched by the saliva of his mouth. For some reason, even that caused her no disgust. Watching her actions with a knowing look, Irungovel read the palm leaf.

"When the time comes for my ideal to be fulfilled, I will begin to speak. If we happen to meet then, I will explain everything. Until then, do not trouble me by asking about my ideal."

— so it was written.

Irungovel raised his head after finishing it! She was looking anxiously at the bitten finger. Blood had clotted there and it had swollen.

"Oh no! Has it swollen? Stay here; I'll bring some medicine!" he said, and hurried out of the mandapam.

He could not find the medicinal leaf he was looking for. Determined somehow to pluck the right leaf and take it back, he searched for a while.

For a short while, mutterings of Chola land—Karikalan—vengeance for vengeance—had left him.

Some vague, indistinct feeling tossed him about. While she was bathing, he had seen her; his heart had begun to melt at that very moment. When she ate the pongal he had cooked, he saw her from very close by. Then he lifted her and whirled her around.

When she fainted... he had begun rubbing the hollow of her chest, and—tch... tch... tch... for a moment he had forgotten that he was a king.

<!-- source boundary: scan 101 → scan 102 -->

His feet carried him in search of the medicinal leaf. But green, fresh thoughts carried him far away, wound him up in a whirlwind of love, and left him struggling, unable to free himself. It could not be said that this flavour of experience was entirely unknown to him.

He had married when he was very young. He had desired and married his maternal uncle's daughter, Perunthevi. For two or three years the two of them had swum and bathed in a great flood of love.

Before their life of happiness could last, a lung disease began to torment Perunthevi.

There was no shortage of good physicians among the Velir people.

Even after all the most accomplished physicians attended to her, there was no improvement. The queen had become nothing but skin and bone.

The appointed hour might slip, but the queen's medicine was never missed. In this condition, Perunthevi merely fulfilled in name the convention that if there is a king, there must also be a queen.

After misfortune befell the country and they came to the forest, need one even ask whether the queen's illness worsened? Thinking of her husband's plight and weeping and weeping, the queen's whole face had become swollen. That swollen face no longer cast even a shadow upon the screen of Irungovel's mind. Only the beauty-filled face of Muthunagai was stirring a great struggle within him.

"Why should she, like a golden statue fashioned by gathering all beauty into one form, wander about alone? Is that male clothing meant only to keep danger from befalling her? Or is it to help her fulfil her ideal? Why does she refuse to speak? Is it not because speaking would reveal a woman's voice? If I had not seen her bathing, even I would have been deceived."

Questions and answers like these arose and were erased at once, and he realized that only her form had been imprinted within him as an indelible picture.
