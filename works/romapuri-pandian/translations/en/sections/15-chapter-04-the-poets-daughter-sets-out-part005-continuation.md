"Dear Father! Forgive me. A conspiracy is under way to tarnish the glory of the great Chola land. Who knows how many people are involved in that very conspiracy. A new state of affairs has arisen in this sacred land, to the point that I have begun to doubt even myself. The enemies are waiting for the right moment to drink the life-blood of Karikala Cholan and to march an army against our motherland and settle their vengeance. Some people here have become their lackeys. Alas! O sacred land of freedom! Is this to be your fate? All night long I thought and thought and shed tears, Father! When I remember that even great men praised as patrons who nurture Tamil have forgotten the very culture that Tamil nurtured, a fire flares in my heart. I asked myself whether I was born in my Chola land. I found the answer: it seems I have a duty to protect the motherland. I have set out to fulfil that duty. I have left with the firm resolve that, in this undertaking, even if my own father stands in my way, even if my dead mother rises and comes to stop me, neither I nor my ideal can be defeated.

"I have work to do until the enemies and traitors of the Chola land are reduced to dust. Until then, do not look for me. If I succeed in my duty and remain alive, I will return and see you. If I give my life in the course of that duty, only Sezhiyan's tiger-claw necklace, which I am taking without your permission, will return to you. That will be the sign that I am dead. Bless me, Father. Farewell."

With love,
Muthunagai

Only after he had become convinced that, however many times he read it, the same letters would still be there did he stop reading the palm leaf. How many times had he pressed Tamil letters to his eyes and thrilled with delight! At such moments he had felt as though flowers were touching his eyes. But now every one of these letters was plunging into his eyes like a stabbing spear. What had caused Muthunagai's sudden change of mind? Was she going to stop the danger that had arisen for the Chola <!-- source boundary: scan 69 → scan 70 -->land? To the poet, everything was one great riddle.

Poor poet! Leaving him to suffer, Muthunagai went straight to the gateway of Karikala Cholan's palace. The guards were astonished by the unprecedented sight of a woman standing there all alone at dawn.

"Who are you, girl? What do you want?" they asked, gathering around her.

"I must see the king urgently!" Muthunagai said in a trembling voice.

At once two warriors went inside and returned after a long while. Muthunagai, waiting anxiously to hear what answer they would bring, received one that delighted her. The Chola king had ordered them to bring her in.

Accompanied by the warriors, Muthunagai walked toward Karikalan's quarters. All along the way she sighed deeply at the sight of the artistic wealth of the motherland brought to life in sculptures and paintings. Gold-polished pillars and ivory lamps fixed upon them made her exclaim, "Ah! What abundance in the Tamil land!" The skins of tigers, lions and leopards slain by the king in battle hung on one side. A crushed jewelled crown hung on a wall. Beneath it were carved the words, "The jewelled crown of the defeated king of Panri country." On a long wall a painting depicting Karikalan's invasion of Lanka stirred a new surge of feeling in the hearts of those who saw it. Beside it, an extremely heavy sword hung from a great pillar. The tip of the sword was broken. Beneath it was inscribed, "This is the sword King Irungovel left on the battlefield when he fled."

The more Muthunagai looked at all these things, the more her anger flared. Her soft lips swelled and trembled as though crying out, "A man of such distinction, one praised as a lion—would he conspire to overthrow the king of his own land?"

At last she entered the mansion where Karikalan was. Karikalan was utterly astonished when he saw her.

"What! You?" he asked in alarm.

"Yes. I need to speak to you alone for a while," said Muthunagai.

The king signalled to the guards and sent them outside. Muthunagai stood looking directly at the king. Karikalan could make no sense of it. "What is it, girl? Why have you come here? What happened? Where is the poet?"

As the Chola king kept piling question upon question, that tender young woman suddenly fell at his feet and cried, "My king! Forgive me!" sobbing aloud.

"Get up, child! Tell me what has happened! Why have you come at this hour of the morning, when dawn has barely broken?" asked the Chola king.

"It has not dawned, Your Majesty; it has not dawned! The darkness surrounding the Chola land has not yet lifted. I came running to tell you that!"

"What are you saying?"

"You have marched armies in every direction and conquered many lands! Countless—countless—enemies have fallen at your feet on hearing the thunder of the Chera army's war drums! You conquered the Oliyar, the Podhuvar and the people of Lanka! You defeated the king of the Velir clan! There is no land that has not praised the glory of your victorious spear! I am not saying that you did not inscribe our emblem upon the Himalayas! But there is one thing, my king! In spite of all this prowess in victory, you have failed to perceive the band of traitors sprouting and growing within your own motherland! Forgive me! I understand that your broad and pure heart simply never thought of those traitors. Yet, Your Majesty, if that band is allowed to grow, the country will perish; our greatness will perish!"

"What! A band of traitors? Who are they? Where are they?"

"They do not live in some separate place! Nor do they gather and live in flocks like crows! Traitors may be among the very servant<!-- verified source split word: scan 71 → scan 72 -->s who attend upon us! They may even be among the commanders who lead armies! They may even be among the poets who string garlands of verse!"

"I do not understand any of this. Tell me a little more clearly, child!"

"Your Majesty, my task is to discover who those traitors are and save the country. That is why I have left home!"

"Then even the poet does not...?"

"Father does not know. Nor must he know. If he knew, he would say I am incapable and stop me. Your Majesty, all the help I ask of you is that you keep this meeting secret!"

"Do you yourself understand what you are saying?"

"A time is coming when I shall prove that some whom you have trusted as splendid peacocks are actually porcupines. This meeting is only the beginning of that, King of Kings! You know, do you not, that Irungovel, who fled after being defeated by you, is roaming about seeking somehow to take revenge on you?"

"Go on. I am listening."

"When you and the Pandiyan king came in procession, Irungovel's commander had come to the capital to murder you by treachery...!"

"Ah! Is that so?"

"Hear the whole of it. He had even taken a spear in hand to kill you. At that moment, a warrior of the Pandiyan country saved you!"

"Who was that warrior?"

"Sezhiyan!"

"What, Sezhiyan? Where is that Sezhiyan now?"

"There is no use asking me beyond that. I do not know where Sezhiyan has been taken. I have left home precisely to learn more. I thought it was my duty to warn you on my way!"

"Child! The man plotting to kill me is Irungovel! I can defeat him with my army. Why should you put yourself through hardship?"

"Can an enemy be defeated by an army alone? Wicked traitors can enter even places where smoke cannot penetrate! Should we not discover who they are?"

"For that?"

"I am saying that I will undertake the work of a spy!"

"To your sense of duty and your love of the motherland, the jewelled crown of this Chola bows in salutation, child! But there is one thing... Will not the great poet who has raised you with such care be tormented with grief?"

"Even about him, Your Majesty, you will hear astonishing news!"

As she said this, her tongue trembled and her eyes filled like pools. Yet she concealed it and mastered herself. The Chola king waited for what more she might say. She changed the subject.

"That is, I mean that Father too may have to undertake spy work like me!" she said, looking at the king's face. A new majesty seemed to blossom upon it. His heart overflowed with pride on hearing that a family devoted to nurturing Tamil was concerned for his life and for the honour of the motherland. Karikalan took pride in the courage of this young Tamil woman who had set out to serve the country without even her father knowing.

"I bless you. Ask for whatever you need for your duty, and I will give it!" he said.

"I need very little. Five or six sets of a warrior's clothing, and a good horse! That is all!"

"You are setting out disguised as a man? Will your voice not betray you?"

"Why should you worry? I may even deceive you!"

Karikalan laughed. He clapped his hands and summoned a bodyguard. He ordered him to take Muthunagai away and, without anyone knowing, give her what she had asked for and send her on her way. As Muthunagai took leave of the Chola king, she begged him not to let this news reach her father's ears. Looking at her kindly, he assured her, "Karikalan does not break his word." Before sending her away, he gave her sound warnings about the dangerous task upon which she was setting out despite all attempts to dissuade her.

After she left, Karikalan was thoroughly confused. Why had Muthunagai become involved in this affair without the poet knowing? The question gnawed at him. Muthunagai's explanation—"Father would stop me! That is why he must not know"—did not seem entirely sufficient. Her statement that he would hear astonishing news even about her father, and the words "splendid peacock" and "porcupine," kept rolling through his mind.

Leaving the king confused and the poet shocked, Muthunagai was now riding at great speed along a forest path on a sturdy horse, dressed as a warrior. Her first objective was to discover where Sezhiyan had been taken. For that reason she wandered here and there along the forest paths. She had heard that Irungovel and his men were roaming the forests, and based her search on that information. After driving the horse on for a long time, both horse and rider were exhausted. At last she dismounted near a spring in the forest. She drank from the spring, let the horse graze, and walked about for a while to stretch her legs.

Then she heard the sound of someone approaching from a short distance away. She stood between two great boulders that looked like rocks arranged to channel the water of the forest stream, and watched intently. A beautiful young woman—a tender vine, golden from head to foot—was swaying gracefully toward the spring.

<!-- source boundary: scan 74 → scan 75 -->

Seeing that the young woman was coming right toward her, Muthunagai ran quickly and lay down on a long rock.

The young bird who had reached the spring saw a warrior lying there asleep. She started. She thought of running away and even tried to do so. But her eyes would not let her leave: like a garment caught on a fence, they had become caught upon the sleeping warrior.

Pretending to sleep can sometimes become a painful ordeal. One cannot know whether the person for whose sake one is feigning sleep is still nearby or has gone away. And the eyes rolling behind closed lids during such play-acting can betray the pretence.

How long can someone pretending to sleep keep drawing and releasing breath in exactly the same rhythm? One can pretend to turn this way and that, slowly open one's eyes at any moment, and see what is happening.

Even that option was denied to Muthunagai. The reason was that there was no room to roll about on the rock where she lay. Nearby there was nothing but the spring, the sound of the waterfall and the stir of the forest. Was the beautiful maiden who had come there still present, or had she gone? Muthunagai was completely confused. The maiden, for her part, did nothing except struggle somehow to wrench her eyes away.

Thus, as far as the two of them were concerned, silence had taken up residence—outwardly! Within, however, the young woman's heart was melting like a flood-swollen stream. Muthunagai could not keep up the act for long. So she too lay there in distress. The moments stretched on.

A new idea now occurred to Muthunagai. To find out whether the young woman was still nearby or had gone away, she devised a trick. 'If I pretend to roll off the rock on which I am sleeping, she may run and catch me. If she runs over and catches me, that means she is still here,' Muthunagai thought. Then, as though stirring drowsily from sleep, she stretched with an "uss" and rolled toward the rock's <!-- source boundary: scan 75 → scan 76 -->edge. Just as she had expected, the young woman ran over and caught Muthunagai before she could fall.

Muthunagai could no longer pretend to be asleep. Rubbing her eyes as though she had slept soundly, she looked around in astonishment, sat up on the rock, and stared wide-eyed in wonder at the woman standing before her.

The young woman stood with her head bowed so low that her blue little eyes seemed reflected in the nail of her big toe. Muthunagai marvelled at her beauty. The loveliness of her hair, curling like waves, her eyes sharp as arrowheads, and her famed slender waist like a streak of lightning made one wonder whether the waterfall itself had taken the form of a woman and come to stand nearby.

Muthunagai waited for a while, expecting the bowed head to rise. But the young woman continued to prop the earth with her eyes. Muthunagai began to hope that she might learn some fresh news through her. At the same time, she was troubled by the puzzle of finding such a maiden in the middle of a forest far beyond the country's borders.

'Who could she be? She looks as though she comes from a well-to-do family... Has her family now fallen on hard times? Why is so much sorrow spread across her face? And what is causing a new flood of feeling, like the blaze of a torch on a theatre stage, to sweep across her features and drive that sorrow away? Is she in love with me? Ayyo! Has she actually taken me for a man? I had no idea this disguise could bring about such a calamity! All right—how am I to learn anything about her?' Question after question arose in Muthunagai's heart.

She opened her mouth to ask, "Who are you, girl?" Then a sudden obstacle stopped her: her voice! What if the young woman realized from it that she was a woman? Muthunagai had not yet learned to alter her voice to sound like a man. After all, she had only just put on the disguise. Would not the disguise and the acting become complete only as she went along?

<!-- source boundary: scan 76 → scan 77 -->

So she did not want to betray herself through her own voice. She decided to behave as though she could not speak. Slowly she went near the young woman and asked by gesture, "Who are you?"

The young woman did not answer. Smiling, her cheeks reddening, she lowered her head without caring even about the ache in her neck and said, "It hurts."

Muthunagai lifted the young woman's chin with her hand, moved her own face expressively, and once again asked in mute gestures who she was.

A shiver ran through the young woman's whole body. She moved away, ran a little distance and stopped. Looking at Muthunagai, she asked in a tone like the vibration of a veena string, "Why, can you not speak?"

At once Muthunagai took one of the palm leaves tucked at her waist and wrote something on it in great haste. She held it out to the young woman. Blushing, the young woman reached out, took the palm leaf and read it.

"I am observing a vow of silence for the sake of an ideal. That ideal will be fulfilled in a little while. Only after that will I be freed from this mute state," the written sentence said, greatly astonishing her.

"What is your name?" she asked.

Muthunagai immediately took the necklace from around her neck and pointed to one of the pearls strung upon it.

"Oh! Your name is Muthu?" the young woman said.

Muthunagai nodded as if to say yes, and then asked with her finger, "What is your name?"

The young woman did not answer at once. Her eyes clouded. Her face darkened. She turned away with a hand on her head. Muthunagai would not leave her alone. She went closer, placed a hand on her shoulder and turned her around. Do you know what delight that <!-- source boundary: scan 77 → scan 78 -->act gave the poor girl?

Yet, still weeping, she looked at Muthunagai and asked, "What is your ideal?" Muthunagai gestured that she could not say.

"I will tell you my name only if you tell me your ideal!" the young woman said through her tears.

Muthunagai bent down and looked at the ground. She looked all around. At last she found what she wanted. Yes—a piece of charcoal! Showing it, she asked in mute gesture, "What is this?"

"Kari," said the beautiful young woman.

Then Muthunagai pointed to her leg. The young woman said, "That is kaal."

Putting the two together, Muthunagai again posed a question through gesture.

"Kari-kaal... Karikaal... Karikaal. Yes—Karikala Cholan! What about him?" the young woman asked eagerly.

Smiling, Muthunagai drew her sword and raised it as though taking revenge.

"Is your ideal to take revenge on Karikala Cholan?" the young woman asked, raising her voice slightly.

Muthunagai jerked her head fiercely as though to say yes, rolled her eyes, and acted with all the passion of vengeance. Nor did she stop there; once again she loosed the old arrow of a question: "Now tell me—what is your name?"

"My name is Thamarai! Do not ask me anything more than that!" Before she could finish saying it, the young woman broke into sobs.

Muthunagai was filled with one fear: that she might suddenly slip and speak aloud. So every moment she kept careful watch over herself. Her conviction that she might obtain some information from Thamarai grew stronger. For when Muthunagai indicated that she was going to take revenge <!-- source boundary: scan 78 → scan 79 -->on Karikala Cholan, she had marked in her heart how a flash of light had appeared and vanished across Thamarai's face. She took out a palm leaf, wrote on it and showed it to Thamarai.

It read:

"Tell me everything about yourself!

Yours,
Muthu"

Thamarai looked at the palm leaf and then gazed up at Muthunagai's face. For a little while their eyes communed. Muthunagai found it amusing. 'What a foolish girl this Thamarai is!' she thought, smiling to herself.

A longing had arisen in Thamarai to pour out everything pent up within her. Her moist lips began to part for that purpose.

At that moment, she heard four or five women calling "Thamarai! Thamarai!" all at once, and started in alarm. Looking at her beloved Muthu, she hurriedly said, "I will meet you here tomorrow, at this same place and this same time," and ran away. Muthunagai remained standing there silently.

After a short while, Muthunagai slowly followed the path Thamarai had taken and watched. She saw Thamarai and four or five other women bathing joyfully without clothes in one part of the waterfall. Muthunagai herself felt a kind of embarrassment and closed her eyes. Perhaps it was the new embarrassment born of Thamarai believing her to be a man! The lovely maidens frolicked, sang and bathed in the waterfall, came ashore, dressed, walked some distance and mounted horses tethered behind the cover of bushes. Muthunagai grew even more puzzled. She too ran back and mounted her horse.

She set out slowly, following the group of women. The horses of Thamarai and the others moved slowly only as they started, then broke into a full gallop. Muthunagai followed behind in such a way that the sound of her horse's hooves would not reach them.

<!-- source boundary: scan 79 → scan 80 -->

As she followed them, her gaze suddenly darted in another direction. What an astonishing sight! She could not believe her eyes. Far away, the familiar "Yavana elder" sat majestically on a tall horse, intently watching the horses of Thamarai and the others, her own horse, and Muthunagai herself. She could go neither forward nor back.

Her suspicion that the Yavana elder must in fact be Irungovel grew stronger. The fear that, if she fell into his hands, none of her aims would be fulfilled threw her into a panic. She struck the horse sharply, dug the tips of her feet into its flanks, and sent it flying at a full gallop.

The Yavana elder did not give up either. He pursued her at tremendous speed. Across forest and high ground the two horses raced like the wind.

She herself had no skill in combat. The fear that the riding she had learned in youth and her little sword practice would be of no use against great warriors like Irungovel made her ride still faster. Could the pounding of the Yavana elder's approaching horse-hooves possibly compete with the sound raised by Muthunagai's own racing heart? It was doubtful!

After racing and circling through all kinds of places, she finally escaped from the Yavana elder.

In a densely wooded place, she and her horse stood without making a sound, and the Yavana elder raced on and lost her. When the sound of the elder's horse-hooves faded, Muthunagai turned her horse in another direction.
