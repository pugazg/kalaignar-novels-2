2

Muthunagai

<!-- source boundary: scan 45 → scan 46 -->

Having thrown Peruvazhuthi's heart into a storm, Sezhiyan rested peacefully in the house of the poet Karikannanar. He greatly liked Muthunagai's affectionate hospitality. He spoke with praise of the pepper-water she had prepared for him, saying it had given him great vitality. Sezhiyan became intensely eager to learn all about her family history.

"What is your mother's name?" he asked.

"Thamaraikkanniyar! But I have never even seen my mother's face! I am a child whose birth killed my mother!" Muthunagai replied, weeping.

Gently, Muthunagai asked Sezhiyan about his mother and father.

"They exist!" Sezhiyan said.

"Where are they?"

"In my heart."

"Your mother's name?"

"Tamil."

"Your father?"

"The Pandiyan country!"

"What are you saying?"

"Yes, Muthunagai! I am the son born of the father that is the Pandiyan country and the mother that is Tamil!"

"Tell me properly, the way I did!"

"I cannot tell you properly! Somehow I must have had a mother and a father; that much is certain!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 46 → scan 47 -->

"Who were they? What were their names?"

"Please don't ask—I cannot tell you! But do not conclude from that that I had no mother or father! They make up stories of babies suddenly appearing in lotuses and lilies; I am no such divine birth! I am a human being born from a mother's womb!"

Muthunagai was delighted by the charm of his speech. Though the memory of her mother had caused her sorrow, the flavour in his replies made her forget that grief to some extent.

While the two were talking, a chant came from the street: "Love is Siva! Love is Siva! Virtue is Saivism!"

Telling Sezhiyan that Siva devotees had arrived, Muthunagai opened the street door. Before she had fully opened it, a group of more than fifteen devotees poured inside in a rush. Muthunagai was utterly taken aback. She was startled and confused to see a group of devotees who had been passing along the street suddenly enter the house.

They paid no attention to her. Crying, "Love is Siva! Virtue is Saivism!" they sprang toward Sezhiyan's bed. They seized him as a hawk seizes its prey.

Sezhiyan struggled and stared. Swords flashed in the hands of the devotees who were chanting "Love is Siva." Muthunagai screamed at the sight. One Siva devotee held her. Another brought a bunch of herbs close to her face. She fainted and fell. No one looked at her again. The group lifted Sezhiyan and left. Muthunagai lay unconscious, unmoving like a statue laid down.

Karikannanar was on his way home, watching the devotees move along the street chanting "Love is Siva" and saluting them as they passed. How could he have discovered that the drugged Sezhiyan had been hidden inside their *anna-kavadi*? <!-- source boundary: scan 47 → scan 48 --> He was hurrying home to tell Sezhiyan and Muthunagai what had happened at the palace.

The moment he entered the house, Karikannanar was jolted. Everywhere he turned there was only disorder. Muthunagai, who always came forward with a smiling face to welcome him, was nowhere to be seen. He could not bear that frightening silence.

"Muthunagai! Muthunagai!" he called. Suddenly the call became a cry. "My child, Muthunagai!" he shouted, rushing to the place where she lay fallen. He lifted his daughter, lost to the embrace of unconsciousness, laid her on his lap, and called in a trembling voice, "Child! Child!" He scooped up tears and sprinkled them on her face. Drops rolled down her face like water falling on a lotus leaf and rolling away in pearl-like beads. Tearing a strip from the black border of his upper cloth, he twisted it into a wick with his hand, lit it at the lamp, extinguished it again, and directed the smoke toward Muthunagai's nose.

After this smoke treatment had been given several times, Muthunagai's head moved slowly. At last her eyelids opened, as though they had opened by themselves to reveal a face of light... her dark pupils moved gently.

"Father!" she said in a trembling voice.

"What happened, child, my dear?" the poet asked in a voice of tears.

She could not speak. Her eyes moved all around. Suddenly she sat up. "Have they taken him away?" she asked in anguish, and sobbed uncontrollably.

"Tell me, child! Tell me everything! I understand nothing at all. Alas! When I saw you lying there, I felt as though all the Tamil grammar I had learned had suddenly vanished from my memory! Tell me, child—what happened? Where is Sezhiyan?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 48 → scan 49 -->

The poet feared what answer his daughter might give. The suspicion tormented him that perhaps Sezhiyan's enemies had taken revenge upon him.

"As usual, I heard the sound of the Siva devotees coming along the street. I was afraid they might wrongly think we were showing them no respect because the door was closed, so I ran and opened it. They came rushing into the house. They surrounded Sezhiyan and tried to carry him away. I tried to stop them. They held some bunch of green leaves near my face. That was all; my head began to spin, and I could feel myself fainting. I could not stand, so I sat down. By then the devotees had lifted him and run away. I got up quickly, fell down with a thud, and after that I knew nothing."

When Muthunagai finished giving this answer, a new confusion arose in the poet Karikannanar. He rose, thinking that he must run at once and tell the news to the Chola Karikalan and Peruvazhuthi Pandiyan. "I shall go to the palace and inform the king immediately," he told his daughter and set out.

But before he had crossed the threshold, he turned back and stood there bewildered.

"Why have you come back, father?" Muthunagai asked eagerly.

"Nothing, child! At first, news came to the kings at the palace that Sezhiyan could not be found. I was there then too. I thought I might tell them that Sezhiyan was in our house. But Sezhiyan's request remained in my heart. I kept silent because I feared I would be going against what he had told me if I revealed the news. If I now go and say that someone has carried Sezhiyan away, they will first ask why I did not say that Sezhiyan had been here. The Pandiyan may be all right, but who can answer King Karikalan, child? The king's question would certainly be just. Had I told them earlier where Sezhiyan was, would this <!-- source boundary: scan 49 → scan 50 -->danger have come upon him now? The fault is mine!" the poet said, blaming himself.

"There is no fault on your part, father; the fault is Sezhiyan's! Was he not the one who first stopped us from informing the kings?" Muthunagai said, consoling her father.

"Who could the men who carried Sezhiyan away be? They must be men of Irungovel, the Velir ruler. In the humiliation of defeat, perhaps he is determined somehow to create disorder in the great Chola country!" the poet muttered.

"They said Irungovel had been defeated by King Karikalan, had lost his country and his city, and was living in hiding somewhere, father. How does he have so many men now?" Muthunagai asked doubtfully.

"The forest protects those who have no country. A tiger may hide in the bushes of that forest—and this mouse of the Velir clan may hide there too. A lion may lie in its caves—and this little fox that nurtures enmity may crouch there as well. There are a few traitors moving within the country itself, seeking to restore authority to that fox and bring down Karikalan, our protector. Everything that happened today must have been their joint effort," Karikannanar said, sparks seeming to fly from his eyes.

"What shall we do, father, if some danger has befallen Sezhiyan's life?"

"That is what I too am thinking about with worry," said the poet, letting his gaze fall to the ground as he thought. His eyes, sent out in thought, noticed something near the side of the bed where Sezhiyan had lain and widened further. The poet went quickly and picked it up. A necklace bearing tiger claws! From the front of that beautifully worked gold plate, tiger claws projected. He examined the necklace closely.

"This is the one Sezhiyan was wearing around his neck!" Muthunagai said, recognizing it.

<!-- source boundary: scan 50 → scan 51 -->

The poet examined the tiger-claw necklace again. Then he gave it to Muthunagai and told her to put it safely in a chest and lock it. Muthunagai did so. She felt an urgent need to rescue Sezhiyan somehow. She had known him only for a short time, yet within that time she had realized that he was a man of very high character. When a beautiful young woman and a young man are left alone together, how many mishaps have budded, flowered, borne fruit and ripened in the name of love! And if one of the two is wounded or ill and circumstances arise in which the other must tend to them, the new impulse called love can spread first through the body and then seize the heart, rather than entering the heart unhindered and only afterward reaching the body.

But Sezhiyan, left in precisely such circumstances, had shown no improper sign of any kind toward Muthunagai, who had treated and cared for him with affection. Muthunagai greatly admired and rejoiced in that noble character. Seeing that quality, she held him in great respect. For that very reason she suffered with the desire to save him somehow from the danger that had befallen him. Once more she pressed her father to find a way to rescue Sezhiyan.

The poet began pacing back and forth like a civet cat, thinking deeply. He lamented what new anguish this was, on a festival day of all days.

At last, looking at Muthunagai, he said, "Do not worry! I have found a roundabout way to get the news about Sezhiyan to the king. The news will reach the king's ear without blame falling on us!"

"What way, father?" asked Muthunagai.

"You must not ask for all the details! You can sleep now without worrying!" he said, raising his voice slightly.

"Which of the two kings are you going to inform? The Pandiyan or the Chola?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 51 → scan 52 -->

"What does it matter to you which one? Sezhiyan must return safely; that is all, isn't it?"

"No, father, not for that reason! I only asked because I wanted to know which one."

"Oh, what a nuisance you are—a great nuisance, Muthunagai! If there is anything to eat, give it to me! Then sleep! Go!"

Muthunagai could not understand why such sudden severity had appeared in her father's speech.

'Perhaps father is inwardly tormented that Irungovel, though in hiding, is creating such confusion in the country. Or perhaps the worry is piercing his mind that, because he did not reveal the news about Sezhiyan at first, what might the king think if he revealed it now? Maybe that is why there is such agitation in father's speech,' Muthunagai thought. Then another fear came to her. Because she kept insisting again and again that Sezhiyan must be saved, perhaps her father might have formed a wrong idea about her—that was the fear.

So she stopped talking. She went to the kitchen, brought fruit and milk, and placed them before her father. He ate the fruit and drank the milk. All the while Muthunagai kept staring at her father's face without taking her eyes away. Karikannanar was not unaware that his daughter was watching him. When he had finished eating, he rose, bolted the street door, and reclined on his bed.

Muthunagai understood nothing. The man who had said he had found a way to send news about Sezhiyan to the king was now making preparations to sleep; the thought troubled her. Fearing that if she remained sitting up instead of going to bed she might make her father's mind still more restless, she too lay down in the veranda. After a little while, varied sounds of breathing began to rise, showing that the poet was asleep. Muthunagai could not sleep at all. She turned over and over.
