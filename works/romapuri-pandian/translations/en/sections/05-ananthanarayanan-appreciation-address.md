In the West there is a proverb about children: "Children should be seen, not heard." It seems to me that the same proverb applies even more strongly to judges. Judges may be seen; outside the courts they should not be heard too much. My conclusion is that retired judges should be still more restrained and modest. There is an expression in our literary tradition: "They brought the man who was in the corner out into the courtyard." I thought for a while: why have they brought me, who was sitting in a corner, out into the courtyard? What qualification do I have? Then one qualification occurred to me. I have had an attachment to Tamil literature since childhood. But there are people here with a greater attachment to literature than I have. There are people who have spent their entire lives in the Tamil literary tradition and in literary research.

When did what we call the novel appear in Tamil? Only in the nineteenth century. (Before that, Beschi, known as Veeramamunivar, had written short prose such as 'Paramartha Guru Kathai'.) Earlier works such as the Iraiyanar Akapporul commentary, the Adiyarkku Nallar commentary and the Parimelazhagar commentary were so difficult that they could hardly be called prose. The book 'Prathapa Mudaliar Charithiram' written by Vedanayagam Pillai—and this must be noted carefully—is comparable in weight to Goldsmith's novel 'Vicar of Wakefield' in English literary history. That is to say, half of it is a moral treatise and the rest is novel. The novel has not yet fully emerged in it. After that, the late Rajam Iyer and my late father Madhaviah wrote, respectively, the two novels 'Kamalambal Charithiram' and 'Padmavathi Charithiram'. It is on the foundation of those novels that novels have now multiplied. <!-- verified source split-word join: scan 12 → scan 13 --> Historical novels in particular have been appearing in Tamil Nadu for the past twenty years. The hundredth year since my father's birth is 1974. On that qualification alone, I think I am fit to release this novel, because it has become established beyond even a little doubt that he was one of the three founders of the novel in Tamil literary history. It would not be proper for me to speak at length in praise of him.

Now we should examine briefly what the rules are for a novel founded on historical evidence. Dr. Kalaignar Karunanidhi, who wrote this, has written a preface. He has shown that he imagined and created this novel 'Romapuri Pandiyan' using particular historical evidence. You may not know this. There was an archaeological researcher called Sir Mortimer Wheeler. While he was at a place called Arikamedu near Puducherry, some gold coins of Augustus Caesar were found in a hoard. That is mentioned in this preface. But another detail is not mentioned in the preface. What one would call a fragment of a pot was also found. It bore the stamp of a workshop from Rome dating to the reign of Augustus Caesar. Sir Mortimer Wheeler wrote that he was greatly astonished when he saw it. That is to say, it becomes clear beyond doubt that shipping and trade between Rome and Tamil Nadu were conducted very rapidly and very actively in that period. Thus this book has been written on the basis of historical evidence. Even so, you should not ask: was it really this Peruvazhuthi Pandiyan in the novel who sent a man called Sezhiyan as an envoy to Rome? What is the historical evidence for that? A novel based on historical evidence must allow room for both the author and imagination. If no room is given to imagination, it becomes a history book rather than a novel. So the first rule is that there must be historical evidence, but there must also be imagination. For example, the novel says that a woman named Geno, belonging to the family of Augustus, learned Tamil from an elderly Yavana woman in Egypt and spoke in Tamil. You should not ask where the historical evidence for that is. It is something that could have happened. Kalaignar has imagined it. Because it accords with what could have happened, we must accept it.

If imagination itself had to remain barren, the book could never have come into being. You should keep this in mind. This is one of the novel's finest features. It contains much historical evidence, yet it has been enlarged by imagination. The imagination is neither outside nor contrary to the historical evidence. One can state a small rule for this. In English it is called *Anachronism*. If I translate it into Tamil, I would say "a contradiction of place, thing and circumstance." There should be no such contradiction of place, thing and circumstance. In a novel based on historical evidence, if one wrote that a Pandiyan of that age drove away in a motor car, we could not accept it. If some scientific fact or other matter unknown to anyone in Rome or Tamil country at that time entered the story, that would be a contradiction of place, thing and circumstance. Kalaignar has written this with great skill without even a trace of such contradictions. But this is a virtue of absence, a kind of virtue defined by the absence of a fault—what one may call a 'Negative Virtue'. You may ask what its positive qualities are. I shall say a little. I do not want to speak at great length; I shall say only a little. Its first outstanding quality is that, though based on historical evidence, it is filled with characters and a 'framework'—what we call a plot—that could entirely have existed; and through this skilfully written book the prosperity of the Tamils of that age, Tamil culture, Tamil character, Tamil valour and the firmness of womanhood are all explained. Some may think: all this is imaginative to some extent; surely there must have been many shortcomings in those days too.

Recently I had to speak to some Americans about the distinction of Tamil Nadu—that is, about the resources and qualities of Tamil Nadu from the second century B.C. to the second century A.D. At that time I read many books, rendered material from Tamil into English, and explained it to them. What struck me most then was this: in Tamil we speak of *agam* and *puram*. The first is the greatness of love—the affection that naturally arises without blemish between a man and a woman after they come of age; the second is valour. Valour in war, valour on the battlefield, and the valour of the mothers of battlefield warriors. These two have become great flavours in Tamil—in Tamil literature. These two are also great flavours in this novel. Therefore I may give it as my judicial opinion that the novelist is not guilty of the fault of exaggeration—or what they would call excessive praise in the northern language.

Yes. There is a woman here called Muthunagai. She goes about in male disguise; she rides a horse without the slightest fear; she becomes caught in terrifying situations. We cannot simply say that these things are not in accord with Tamil tradition. If I begin giving examples from literature, it will take time. I told those Americans an example from the Purananuru. In one poem a mother says: "Are you looking for my son? My son was a tiger that stayed for a short time in the cave that was my womb. (What a beautiful simile!) That tiger has gone! You may see him lying face-up on the battlefield, without a wound on his back."

Thus there can be no doubt at all that *maram*—martial valour—and heroism are deeply concentrated qualities in Tamil Nadu. Another thing struck me as especially interesting. Can a woman disguise herself as a man, and can another woman fall in love with the woman who is in male disguise? Can she mistake her for a man and fall passionately in love? As far as I could recall, this forms an important situation in two plays by the great poet Shakespeare—'As you like it' and 'Twelfth Night'. A woman assumes male disguise. Another woman falls in love with the woman dressed as a man! But Shakespeare left one thing out. Compared with our Kalaignar, Shakespeare seems to have been a little inattentive. What about the voice giving her away? No matter how she dresses and acts as a man, might she not be identified by her voice? Kalaignar has paid very close attention to that point and written, "This woman Muthunagai practised speaking in a male voice." I was a little astonished. Good heavens, I thought, what a good thing he did not become a lawyer and come to the courts; he would have caused us great trouble! He had noticed with such precision even what the great Shakespeare had failed to notice. I truly admired that. I must mention one other thing. Here I am obliged to use a French expression. Some may not know it. I shall give its Tamil meaning. With this new literary grammar, I have had to struggle a little.

In new literary circles the French expression '*avamakaam*' is used. It means 'vanguard class'. One important characteristic of a vanguard-class writer is sexual or gender feeling—that is, a man being attracted to a woman, and a woman being attracted to a man. <!-- verified source continuation: scan 15 → scan 16 --> They write about it openly—so openly that, if you took it home, you could not show it to your children, your wife, or your sisters. This is great literature! Or a sign of great literature. I do not know how this idea arose. Nor do I know what there is in it. For this sexual feeling is something we share equally with animals. But a human being has a mind. He has life and thought, and across the difference between male and female he can give his whole love. In love that gives itself wholly, pure love includes a share of this sexual feeling. Then it has a beauty and radiance of its own. If that is separated out and expressed merely as raw feeling, openly and explicitly, it becomes very lacking in taste and very lacking in savour. I still do not understand why they regard that as literature. It genuinely leaves me perplexed.

In this novel written by our Kalaignar, the flavour of love—what is called *sringaram* in the northern language—is fundamental. Pathos (*sokarasam*), the flavour of love (*sringaram*), and humour (*hasyam*): it is not good to create literature without these three. He has taken up the flavour of love, but he has handled it only as pure love. In that love there is sexual feeling, attraction and allure, but they are submerged and concealed within it. You may—speaking as a judge, I tell you—give this book freely to your younger sisters, wife and children. There is not one sentence, not even one word, that would give them an obscene feeling or cause embarrassment. I must praise this. Some say, no, I will write it openly and explicitly; we have the right to do so. (These days everything is so confused that one cannot tell who has what right!) They ask both for fundamental rights and for the right to strike or beat. Is there a fundamental right? Is there a right to beat? Where will this end? It is perverse.

Kalaignar has handled very pure love in this novel. In that respect too, the book deserves special praise. I can say one thing about the prose style. A judge cannot deliver a judgment without finding at least one fault. His prose is written very simply, but he has adhered very closely to literary tradition. Therefore, in some places, <!-- verified source continuation: scan 16 → scan 17 --> I wonder whether everyone will possess enough knowledge of Tamil. In some places he has used expressions from the Sangam age itself. In some places he writes 'kazharinar'. There are so many ways to say "said," "spoke," or "told"; only those who have read some literature will know the word 'kazharinar'. That is one thing I must say. There is one other thing, and I shall say it last. This is something I discovered myself. I think I can say this and conclude.

At times I wonder whether, to some extent, we are mistaken in the way we think about the imagination of an individual. The ocean of imagination does not belong only to one individual. This ocean of imagination spreads across the whole world. It exists in every human heart. So when we see an idea of a poet who lived in one century shining in the poem of another poet in another century who knew nothing at all about the first, I wonder whether the poetic mind belongs to no single person. I shall give a small illustration. I take that illustration from the work called Kalinkattupparani, which our Kalaignar likes very much. It was written by Seyankondan, the court poet of Kulothunga Cholan. When he describes the battlefield, this verse occurs:

"See the Earth-maiden, unable to leave her lord's body,  
Bear it in her own arms; before the maidens of heaven  
Can embrace that departing life,  
She lets her own life go with his—see her, see!"

He describes that chaste queen, Bhoomadevi, taking her husband's corpse into her own lap because she cannot let it lie upon the earth, and giving up her own life along with his lest, when his life goes to the heavenly world, the celestial maidens should embrace it. This poet lived in the eighth century, in the time of Kulothunga Cholan—I do not remember the history exactly; I have forgotten it—and there is not the slightest doubt about that. In the fifteenth or sixteenth century, the great poet Shakespeare wrote the play 'Antony and Cleopatra'. He did not even know who Seyankondan was. He did not know Tamil. He did not know India. In his play, Cleopatra has a serpent bite her on the breast and kills herself. And Jaraath, her maid, too...
