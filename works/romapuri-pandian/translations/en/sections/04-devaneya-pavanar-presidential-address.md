Today, on the occasion of the golden jubilee of Arutselvanar, our honourable Tamil Nadu Chief Minister Karunanidhi, we are holding the presentation—or release—function of two books he has written: the novel "Romapuri Pandiyan" and a collection of essays titled "The Gentle Breeze that Blew on the Stage."

Of these two books, the novel 'Romapuri Pandiyan' will be released by our retired Chief Justice, the honourable Ananthanarayanan. Pavarasu Kannadasan will receive it. After that, the collection of essays will be released by the honourable scholar K. Govindanar, and the eminent scholar Ki. Va. Jagannathan will receive it. There is no need for me now to say anything special about this novel, 'Romapuri Pandiyan', because a great cloud of eloquence is waiting here to speak.

It is a *pudhinam*—that is, a novel—a novel written with great skill on a historical foundation. It is a new and expansive story. The story is told beginning with the founding of Rome. That is said to have been in 753 B.C. When they think of this, some people—those without research—may even wonder whether it is really that ancient.

But Tamil is older than that. Tamil Nadu had not only trade relations with Rome; to go further, it had a linguistic relationship even before that. It is not merely that the names of certain goods connected through that trade are Tamil; many basic words in that language itself would be Tamil. There is no time to tell you all of them now. <!-- verified source continuation: scan 9 → scan 10 --> I shall tell you on another occasion if the opportunity arises. This is the story of Romapuri Pandiyan. Our Chief Minister has written it with great skill. When an epic in the form of a novel is written, its story may be described as falling into four types.

Here he has taken a real person as the hero and beautifully woven together what existed and what did not. Looking at this, one feels that he was born with the talent for it. One might even be led to believe that Karikala Valavan himself had been born again as our Karunanidhi. Karikala Valavan too was born in this very Kaveri country. So was he. Karikala enriched Poompuhar; he too is now renewing it. Karikala built banks for the Kaveri; he too is trying as far as he can to build a protective bank for the waters of the Kaveri. In his youth Karikala was beset and tormented by enemies and escaped from them. In the same way, everyone knows that in the last general election he too somehow fell into the hands of his enemies and escaped. Even the names invite comparison: the first sound in Karikalan and the first sound in Karunanidhi are somewhat alike. If only there had been a photograph or painted portrait of Karikalan in those days, we might even wonder whether their facial features too would resemble one another. To that extent, his whole heart, and all three—his knowledge, memory and intellect—are directed toward renewing Poompuhar and enriching the Kaveri.

He is now, by virtue of office, in the position of a king—the position of a ruling king.

Fearlessness, generosity, wisdom and energy—these four,  
Without any one lacking, are a king's qualities.

Wakefulness, learning and resolute courage—these three  
Must never leave the one who rules the land.

Let deliberation reach its conclusion and become resolve;  
For that resolve to remain trapped in delay is harmful.

Delay what may rightly be done with delay; do not delay  
The deed that must be done without delay.

When we look at many Tirukkural couplets such as these, he stands as an excellent literary illustration of them all. In ancient times there were three academies in the Pandya country for the nurturing of Tamil. <!-- verified source continuation: scan 10 → scan 11 --> In the first academy, seven ascended the poetic stage. *Paa* means verse. In the second academy, in the middle period, five ascended the poetic stage. In the last academy, three are said to have done so. Many of you will know that two years ago, in this very place, he too ascended the poetic stage. It was there that the title "Tamilvel" was conferred on him by professors. On that day, not only from across Tamil Nadu but even from the Dravidian lands, Tamil professors had gathered here. On their behalf the title "Tamilvel" was conferred on him. We must indeed say that he ascended the poetic stage there. All his poems were published, in both Tamil and English, in an excellent research-oriented manner. Now comes the next stage: prose. In ancient times, among our poets, usage was entirely in verse. It was ordinary people who, like us, spoke in the form called speech—prose, what is called 'prose'. The others—that is, the poets—not only wrote in verse, they even spoke in verse in those days! In English today they distinguish 'colloquial' and 'literary'. We now say ordinary usage and literary usage. But in ancient times they spoke only of ordinary usage and verse usage. When I speak of the ancient people, I wish to say one thing.

They lived in Kumari Nadu, which was submerged in the south. Tamils did not come from a foreign land, as some people think! They are the ancient inhabitants of the south; Tamil too arose in the south. If someone had researched Tamil properly—researched it on a historical basis—they would know its truth. If anyone says that Tamil came from abroad, one can reach only one of two conclusions: either they do not know Tamil properly, or they are enemies of Tamil. Tamil is a language that arose in the south. Therefore it existed even before this connection with Rome. As for the history of Tamil Nadu and this Roman connection, the Roman date here is the eighth century B.C. Even before that there were relations with Egypt and Sumer.

Next, the novel 'Romapuri Pandiyan' will now be released on this stage.
