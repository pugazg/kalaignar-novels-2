Receiving the first copy of the book  
Address delivered by **Kaviyarasu Kannadasan**

I am very happy to receive the first copy of this book, 'Romapuri Pandiyan', released here by Justice Mr. Ananthanarayanan.

The judge who spoke before me has said, with all the clarity with which it needed to be said, everything about this book. "An argument after judgment is not reasonable. It cannot be accepted. Here he himself made the arguments for the lawyers on both sides and he himself delivered the judgment. Therefore, what he said is the complete and final true assessment."

Kalaignar Karunanidhi has long shown an interest in writing historical novels. For him, and for those like him who have a love of Tamil, it is enough if somewhere in history there is a trace saying that so-and-so lived at such-and-such a time; an urge drives them to follow those people and, if possible, go all the way to Rome and give that history a new polish. Beyond that, something indispensable to any writer is that, when he sees one thing, another should arise in his mind. Because this does not happen to most writers, when we read their writing, they themselves stand before us rather than their writing. But in his case, when he sees one object, another occurs to him.

For one thing to unfold into another when one sees it is the natural right of a good imaginative writer. Otherwise, a man looking at a woman would call an eye merely an eye and a mouth merely a mouth. If it occurs to one person to call the eye a water-lily and the mouth a kovai fruit, then one thing is expanding into another. It expands a little more imaginatively.

Thus, taking as his basis the fact that there was a relationship between Rome and our country, and gathering whatever historical evidence he could find, Kalaignar has written it as a fine, large novel. There is a difficulty in writing a historical novel. However many characters we take up, we must keep remembering where each of them is. Otherwise, when we write the next part, everything will turn upside down.

I began writing an epic called 'Pandimadevi'. It has been twelve years since I began that epic. Its title is Pandimadevi. The one I went on following was Cheramadevi. She is in Lanka now. Not knowing how to bring her back, for twelve years Pandimadevi has been standing in the middle of the street. When carrying characters forward, one must keep them in memory as one goes. If the Pandiyan goes to Rome while the queen remains here in Tamil Nadu, the events between the two must be drawn while keeping that in mind. So, when following characters such as those I mentioned earlier, one must follow them very carefully. Above all, characterization and the qualities of the characters must be regarded as very important. Kalaignar has handled that very beautifully in 'Romapuri Pandiyan'. In general, a human being may have nine qualities. But when a person is created as a character, he must have one quality. Likewise, if you look at any outstanding novel or play, that character must remain one.

To show that, while creating characters, the form of the story itself should be visible before one's eyes, I read nearly 200 pages of 'Romapuri Pandiyan'. It serves as good evidence. In Tamil there are countless stories about the heroic traditions of the people of Tamil Nadu. The way Tamil people lived in those days is shown to us by the spheres of *agam* and *puram*. This book by Kalaignar Karunanidhi brings both *agam* and *puram* to fullness. The whole life of the Tamil of that age lay within these two spheres. So he has shown it all clearly here. For this too, like me, he wrote the story week by week in a magazine. When this week's instalment—

is stopped, what we think of as the weakness of the story writer—the serial-story writer—and the weakness of the public is this: next week's magazine sales depend on the point where the story is stopped.

The most important reason the serial written by Kalaignar Karunanidhi stands upright without falling and struggling back up is the strong historical background he took up.

History says that Tamils went to Rome. He imagines how many such people there might have been and what they might have been like. From the fact that only he knows how to stand on the Poompuhar shore and imagine how it might have been before, one may suppose that, when he read ancient history, he imagined how it must have been and how they might have travelled. He may have taken pride in that.

Indeed, when I went to Singapore, I read its history. They have written when Singapore arose. A Tamil king who had been ruling Indonesia, driven from there by another Tamil king who was his enemy, came to that island and saw a lion there. He named it Singapuram. I saw this in a historical note issued by the Singapore government, which says that this is the island's earliest history. Not only that: the history of Malaysia says that the first person there to become a Muslim was a Tamil king who ruled Malacca.

Thus, wherever the Tamil went, he sowed seed. But he returned without harvesting it. That is the betrayal he committed against us. Thus his history exists everywhere. In Cambodia there is an enormous temple. Elsewhere there is no temple for Brahma; there is one there. They bring all the pictures from it and print and sell them in Malaysia. The churning of the ocean of milk, the poison that emerged—everything is there! Its circumference is nearly a mile. It is such a huge temple. The people who were building that temple were sculptor-architects from Tamil Nadu. Cambodian history says that they were the builders. But whether they left it unfinished because of a disease like Kaveri—

<!-- source boundary: scan 21 → scan 22 -->

or for some other reason, history records that it is not known. Thus, when we look across all of Southeast Asia, we can see how our civilization spread and how far our kings went there.

Today we hear histories such as Kadaramkondan and Gangaikondan. And going to Rome from Tamil country was very convenient. If the monsoon wind blew favourably, you could lower the sail and remain quiet. It would go straight to Italy and nowhere else. So they travelled with the help of the monsoon wind. Seeing this as history, our friend Karunanidhi, while writing week by week, did not himself know whether it would become such a large book. He wrote week by week. At times even Kalki would publish a correction: "From what I wrote last week, read this passage this way." He, without doing that, wrote it twice in two different magazines and made it into a good book. This book too is proof that, even amid so much work, his pen continues to run clearly.

I am very happy to receive this first copy of the book. For the first thing I ever received from Kalaignar was the epithet "poet". At the time I had written only two poems. He took me to Pollachi and said, "Poet Kannadasan will speak." The moment he said that, even I felt rather strange.

Thus I received that title from him then; now I receive this book. I can receive many such books from him. Even if this office is lost, the pen in his hand will function as a whirlwind sweeping across this country. Even if someone takes this office away, that pen itself will deal with them. With my wishes that such a powerful pen continue, I receive this.

Vanakkam.
