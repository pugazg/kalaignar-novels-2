We must not become too close! We should see each other often and be happy. That is all. Time and circumstances will soon turn in our favor. After that, we can fulfil our love. All right?" Muthunagai said, raising her head.

Thamarai looked at her and said, "Why impose all these cruel conditions right now? My brother will not act against my wishes. Come without fear," and tugged at her hands.

Then she and Thamarai mounted separate horses. Muthunagai rode behind Thamarai.

From behind a distant thicket, Irungovel watched the direction in which the two women rode. When the sound of the horses' hoofbeats faded away, he mounted his own horse and set off.

The layout of the wooden palace built in the middle of the forest, and the warriors' quarters that seemed to adjoin it, increased Muthunagai's anger toward Irungovel. The thought that the enemy king had built his fortress of intrigue with so many arrangements made her tremble slightly. We have come right into the enemy's dwelling. The satisfaction of having discovered an enemy camp that no one else could find blossomed on her face. But alongside it came the doubt of when her disguise might fall away, and the fear of how terrible the consequences would be if it did; at once they withered her blossoming face.

When rain and wind had competed to do their work in the ruined mandapam and her disguise had fallen away before him, she had found a kind of delight even in that crisis. She had blessed the circumstance. But if such a circumstance arose in the surroundings of this wooden palace? Ayyo! She could not even bear to think of it. She had formed so harsh a judgment of Irungovel. A man who had plotted to kill King Karikalan was not worthy of the

<!-- source boundary: scan 135 → scan 136 -->

respect due a king, she concluded; nor, in her view, could he be a man of Tamil culture. Because Karikala Peruvalathan had become nothing less than a deity to her, she would not hesitate to take revenge on anyone opposed to that deity. She decided with inward joy that meeting Veerapandi had helped her plan, that the meeting had brought a new flavor into her life, and that it was a reward granted to her for service to the country.

As they drew nearer and nearer to the front of the palace, Thamarai kept looking at Muthunagai and smiling. The two horses were moving side by side.

"From now on, only our eyes must speak to each other!" Thamarai said softly.

"Yes! That is exactly what I told you then," said Muthunagai.

"This palace stands in open ground; there are secret places here too where we can meet!" said Thamarai.

Those words sent a shiver through Muthunagai. "All right, all right," she signalled, telling her to stop talking.

After tying the horses at the entrance, Thamarai took Muthunagai straight to where her sister-in-law was. Queen Perunthevi, still in her bed, welcomed the mute physician and asked him to sit down.

"This is my sister-in-law. She who is wasting away in this palace is the lamp of the Velir clan. It is your responsibility as her physician to keep the light of this lamp from dimming!" Thamarai said, looking at Muthunagai.

Muthunagai nodded in agreement, took the queen's hand and began to feel her pulse.

"Why, Thamarai! You said he was mute; his hearing is perfectly good!" Perunthevi asked in surprise.

"He was not born mute, sister-in-law! It happened later!" Thamarai managed. After finishing the pulse examination, Muthunagai looked up at the sky as though

<!-- source boundary: scan 136 → scan 137 -->

she had been thinking about something, then rose from her seat.

"What do you need, physician?" Thamarai asked.

Muthunagai gestured as though she needed nothing and kept pacing back and forth with her head bowed.

"See, sister-in-law! How deeply he is thinking!" Thamarai said, taking comfort from her sister-in-law's answering smile.

As she paced, Muthunagai took a palm leaf and wrote several things on it. She gave it to the queen. Queen Perunthevi received it, read it and handed it to Thamarai. Muthunagai had written on the palm leaf: "This illness can certainly be cured. I need some important herbs for it. Meanwhile, it would be good if I could meet the king!"

Perunthevi remembered what King Irungovel had told her earlier. He had instructed her, "Tell the physician that the king is not in the palace and has gone somewhere." So the queen immediately replied to Muthunagai, "Meeting the king is impossible. He is not here. I do not know where he has gone, nor can I say when he will return!"

Muthunagai looked at Thamarai's face, because Thamarai had told her that the king was there. Seeing Muthunagai's confusion, Thamarai moved her eyes to signal, "The queen is lying." Muthunagai felt a new surge of enthusiasm. Her joy knew no bounds at finding, within the enemy's own household, a means that could work in her favor.

"Go and make whatever arrangements the physician needs, dear," the queen told Thamarai. Thamarai took Muthunagai elsewhere; that place too was full of women. They all stared intently at Muthunagai, glanced sideways at Thamarai, and exchanged all sorts of thoughts among themselves.

Thamarai brought fine guavas on a beautiful plate, placed them before Muthunagai and insisted that she eat. After finishing them, Muthunagai pretended to grow sleepy. Thamarai placed uncovered pillows on a long wooden plank lying there and told her to rest on it. Muthunagai was not surprised at the hospitality shown to her as a royal physician. Nor was she shocked by the magnificence of the bed given to her as the princess's lover.

Muthunagai did not frown and ask, "Is a wooden plank and uncovered pillows all that a palace guest gets?" The reason was that even the bed on which crowned Queen Perunthevi lay was made of rough, coarse wood. It had no turned legs. No ivory had been set into it. There was no soft silk-cotton mattress there. Rough wooden planks had simply been stacked together, and the queen lay on them. As Muthunagai lay there letting her thoughts wander, she felt a little pity for Irungovel's family.

She had heard that, among all the kings of the Velir line, Irungovel had lived with the greatest luxury. The teeth visible in the open mouths of the lions standing on either side of his throne were all said to be diamonds! The lions themselves were said to be gold!

Muthunagai's heart softened as she thought of the condition into which they had now fallen.

Thamarai, who had gone away, came back and asked, "Do you need anything else?" Muthunagai closed her eyes, drew her breath heavily and pretended to sleep. "Poor thing, exhausted and fast asleep!" Thamarai murmured as she moved away.

Thamarai too was caught in an intense inner struggle. 'How am I to reveal to my brother the love that has arisen between Muthu and me? If I tell him, what if something disastrous happens

<!-- source boundary: scan 138 → scan 139 -->

because of it? With all the confusion already troubling my brother's mind, if I stir up another storm, will he be able to bear it?'

Walking slowly, she went toward her sister-in-law's bed. The queen's cough had grown somewhat worse. The maids rushed to her and busied themselves with treatment. Thamarai leaned her sister-in-law against her own chest and rubbed her gently so that the coughing would not trouble her too badly.

Though the sound of coughing reached Muthunagai, who was lying in another place adjoining the queen's quarters, she did not get up because of it and continued to lie there as though asleep.

The cough tormented the queen for a long time before subsiding a little.

"Even death is taking its time coming to take me away!" the queen said with tears in her eyes.

"Do not say such things, sister-in-law! You will not die; you must not die; we will not let you die," Thamarai said, her voice trembling.

Listening closely to this sorrowful scene, Muthunagai wiped away the tears that had welled in her eyes. Women cannot bear to see women weep. But should she lose heart at the tears of women in an enemy's household? A fierce struggle arose between the thoughts urging Muthunagai to harden and strengthen her heart and her compassionate nature. For one moment duty would win; the next, her compassionate heart would win. She lay there torn between the two. Again the queen began to cough violently. The coughing was so severe that it seemed her breathing might stop.

Muthunagai rose from her bed and peered into the queen's room. No one noticed her looking. Seeing the queen writhe and collapse in unbearable distress, and Thamarai droop and falter along with her, Muthunagai was moved by the depth of family affection.

"Ayyo! Must this family suffer like this? If they were in the palace, a thousand physicians would have come by now!" she lamented.

The queen, somewhat relieved from her distress, lay exhausted against Thamarai's chest. Thamarai sat with tears in her eyes, gently patting the queen's shoulder with her soft hands. Just then a warrior entered and stood there after bowing.

"What news?" Thamarai asked eagerly.

"We have Sezhiyan locked up there; it would be good to open the place once and look in, Princess!" the warrior said anxiously.

"Why? What for?" Thamarai asked. By then the queen too had slowly opened her eyes and was listening to the warrior.

"There has been no sound at all from inside that prison. I suspect the man may have died!" said the warrior.

"Then let him die!" the queen said indifferently.

"No, Your Majesty! It is better for us if he stays alive. The king plans to use him to recover the lands we lost!" the warrior replied.

By now, sweat had broken out all over Muthunagai's body.

"What do you say we should do?" the queen asked.

"We need permission to open that cage once and look inside. And if you also have the key to the cage..." The warrior let his words trail off.

"Opening the cage when the king is away..." the queen replied doubtfully.

"I will go myself, open it and look, then come back, sister-in-law!" Thamarai said. The queen agreed, took the key from beneath her pillow, handed it to Thamarai and sent her off with the warning, "Be careful!"

Watching Thamarai leave, Muthunagai looked all around, eager somehow to go with her and learn Sezhiyan's condition. There was no

way. She would have to pass the place where the queen lay. Deciding that whatever happened would happen, Muthunagai crossed that space and followed Thamarai.

Seeing this, the queen stopped Muthunagai. "Stop!! Stop!"

Muthunagai stared at her, understanding nothing.
